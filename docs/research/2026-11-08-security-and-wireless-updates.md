# Sign one package, flash both boards safely

> **Published on 2026-11-16.** Written on 8 November 2026 and kept out of the repo until the maintainer decided to publish its possibly novel ideas openly. Unchanged since then apart from links into the repo, the final section, and decision 19, which now points to the [novelties](../novelties/README.md). Read it as background: where a [decision](../decisions/README.md) says otherwise, the decision wins.

Research date: 8 November 2026. Sources are cited inline. **[unverified]** marks anything not confirmed against a primary source during this research. **Estimate** marks design arithmetic, not measurement. "Measured" means measured on our robot in stage 1. Nothing in this report was built, flashed or run on the robot. **[IRREVERSIBLE]** marks every step that permanently changes a chip.

The robot can meet D2, D3 and S12 without changing any hardware and without burning a single eFuse. It needs two one-time steps first: our own camera firmware has to go onto the ESP32-S3 over USB, and the UNO's bootloader has to be replaced once over ICSP. Today the UNO can't be flashed wirelessly because the stock Optiboot 4.4 runs only after an **external reset**, and nothing on the robot can produce one. The Upload/Cam switch is not the obstacle. In Cam the ESP32 already has a full two-way UART path to the UNO. Upstream **Optiboot 8.3** fits the same 512 bytes, keeps the stock fuses and accepts a request from the application, so the UNO's own firmware can restart into the bootloader on command and the ESP32 can act as its programmer. On the ESP32-S3 the factory partition table already has **two 3.2 MB OTA slots**, and the Arduino core ships with rollback enabled. But the core confirms every new app at boot unless the firmware overrides one hook, so real crash protection takes one deliberate line of code. Trust should come from **one signed release manifest** that lists both images with their hashes, versions and compatibility. The ESP32 checks it before writing anything. The ATmega328P has no cryptography, so the ESP32 is its gatekeeper, and USB stays open as the owner's physical-presence path. Hardware Secure Boot, flash encryption, anti-rollback and download-mode lockdown are all irreversible. They would lock forks and the owner out of their own robot and remove USB recovery and debugging, so the recommendation is to **burn nothing now**. For the link, the robot is paired once over USB: the computer tool writes the Wi-Fi credentials and a random 32-byte per-robot key, and that key then authenticates every command. The recommended path is **option A now** (the UNO over USB, the camera board over Wi-Fi once our firmware runs on it) and **option B as soon as possible** (the UNO over Wi-Fi after the ICSP step). Both run into a real conflict in the plan. D2, D3 and S12 are stage 2 requirements, but decision 0004 keeps the stock camera firmware until stage 3, and the stock firmware can do none of this.

## Option A now, option B as soon as possible

Both options keep the stock hardware. They differ in how the UNO gets its firmware, and option B adds one ICSP session.

| | **Option A: now** | **Option B: as soon as possible** |
|---|---|---|
| UNO update | USB cable, switch in Upload, stock Optiboot 4.4 untouched | Over Wi-Fi, through the ESP32, with the switch left in Cam |
| Camera board update | First flash over USB-C, then signed OTA with rollback | Same |
| One-time steps | First USB flash of our camera firmware | The same, plus one ICSP session that installs Optiboot 8.3 |
| Bootloader baud | 115200 (stock) | 250,000 if the bench test passes, otherwise 115200 with the ESP32 matched to the AVR's real rate |
| If power fails mid-update | Re-run the USB upload. The bootloader survives | The bootloader survives. A page-write order keeps a half-written image from running, and the ESP32 keeps the previous UNO image to re-flash |
| Requirements met | D3, S12 and D16 for the camera board. **D2 isn't met** | D2, D3, S12, D15 and D16 |
| Earliest date | UNO: now. Camera board: when our camera firmware runs (stage 3 under [0004](../decisions/0004-own-protocol-and-control-page.md)) | The ICSP step and the UNO side can be built and tested over USB in stage 2. The wireless leg needs our camera firmware |
| Hardware change | None | None. ICSP connects to existing pins and modifies nothing |

**The plan conflict comes first, because it decides the timing.** The requirements put D2, D3 and S12 in stage 2 ([requirements](../requirements.md)). Decision 0004 keeps the stock camera firmware running until stage 3 ([0004](../decisions/0004-own-protocol-and-control-page.md)). The stock bridge only forwards text in braces to the UNO at 9600 baud ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md#working-with-the-stock-bridge)), so it can't carry a bootloader protocol, and it has no signed update path we control. Either D2, D3 and S12 move to stage 3, or a minimal camera firmware (Wi-Fi, OTA, the UNO programmer and a link bridge) comes forward into stage 2. Both are reasonable. The second meets the requirements as written, but it starts stage 3 work early.

**Option B in order (estimate of the sequence, not a tested procedure).** First, with the maintainer's OK and the tracks off the ground, read and save the UNO's fuses, lock bits and EEPROM over ICSP, which completes the D6 backup. The stage 1 backup lacks those three because reading them needed an ISP programmer ([UNO backup](../notebook/2026-11-04-backing-up-the-uno.md)). Then write Optiboot 8.3 without touching the fuses. Second, give the UNO firmware an authenticated "enter bootloader" command, and test it over USB in stage 2 with avrdude's `-x noautoreset`, which stops avrdude toggling the reset line on port open ([avrdude 8.1 manual](https://avrdudes.github.io/avrdude/8.1/avrdude_6.html)). Third, once our camera firmware exists, the ESP32 verifies a signed package, stores the UNO image, sends the command and programs the UNO over the existing UART. Option A stays available throughout, because Optiboot 8.3 still speaks the same protocol over USB.

**A cheaper bridge exists if the ICSP step slips.** The stock Optiboot 4.4 does enter on an external reset, and the UNO's reset button produces one. Once our camera firmware runs, the ESP32 could keep sending sync bytes while someone presses that button. That is how esp-link catches Optiboot after its own reset pulse: it repeats sync every 70 to 80 ms and gives up after about 5 s ([esp-link](https://github.com/jeelabs/esp-link/blob/master/UC-FLASHING.md)). This needs no ICSP session and arguably meets D2's check ("flash it with no cable connected"), but it needs a hand on the robot. Whether the shield leaves the UNO's reset button reachable is **[unverified]**.

## A one-time bootloader swap replaces the missing reset wire

### The switch isn't the problem. The reset is

One project note claims that the ESP32 can't flash the UNO because of the Upload/Cam switch. **That is wrong.** The switch breaks only the UNO's RX line. In Cam, D0 is driven by the ESP32's TX, and D1 (the UNO's TX) reaches the ESP32's GPIO3 in both positions ([pin map](../hardware/pin-map.md), [camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md)). In Cam, the ESP32 can therefore hold a full conversation with the UNO's bootloader. The switch would block it only in Upload. The real obstacle is getting into the bootloader. Optiboot 4.4 reads the reset cause and runs `if (!(ch & _BV(EXTRF))) appStart();`, so watchdog, brown-out and power-on resets all go straight to the application ([ArduinoCore-avr optiboot.c](https://github.com/arduino/ArduinoCore-avr/blob/master/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)). Software can clear the reset-cause bits but can't set the external-reset bit, and the camera board's 4-pin link carries RX, TX, ground and 5 V with **no reset line** ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md)). Every existing Wi-Fi-to-AVR flasher solves this with a wire from a spare GPIO to RESET. esp-link uses GPIO0 or GPIO12 ([esp-link FLASHING](https://github.com/jeelabs/esp-link/blob/master/FLASHING.md)), the ESP32 OTA_update_AVR project uses GPIO19 ([laukik-hase](https://github.com/laukik-hase/OTA_update_AVR_using_ESP32)), and ESP-UARTArduinoFirmwareUpdate needs "a GPIO wired to the ATmega's reset pin" ([pkerspe](https://github.com/pkerspe/ESP-UARTArduinoFirmwareUpdate)). The camera board's spare GPIOs reach only module pads and test points ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md)).

The options compare as follows.

| Approach | How the UNO enters its bootloader | Cost | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| USB only (option A) | The USB chip's reset line, switch in Upload | A cable and a hand on the switch | **Now** |
| Stock bootloader plus the reset button | A person presses RESET while the ESP32 sends sync | A hand on the robot every time. Button access **[unverified]** | Interim bridge |
| **Optiboot 8.3, app-requested entry (option B)** | The UNO firmware restarts into the bootloader on command | One ICSP session; our own ESP32 programmer | **Recommended target** |
| urboot u8.0 (MiniCore 3.x's bootloader), forked | Same, after editing its entry check | Maintaining a fork; a different upload protocol; vector-bootloader fuses | Only if Optiboot fails |
| Self-update inside the UNO app | The app writes flash through the bootloader's write routine | No room for a second copy. Fragile | No |
| Wire from an ESP32 pad to RESET | A real external reset | **Hardware change.** Soldering to module pads | Stage 5, flagged below |
| UNO R4 WiFi migration | Its own update path | **Hardware change** | Stage 5 |

**Optiboot 8.3 is the best fit.** The current upstream source is v8.3 and says "Skip all logic and run bootloader if MCUSR is cleared (application request)", so an application can enter it on purpose. By default it starts the app after any reset except a plain external one ([Optiboot optiboot.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)). It still fits 512 bytes, and it still speaks the skeleton STK500v1 protocol that `avrdude -c arduino` uses ([Optiboot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)). The UNO firmware can therefore restart itself into the bootloader. To keep that restart safe, it should go through a real watchdog reset, so every pin is reset first. A direct jump would keep the app's pin states ([Arduino forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/entering-bootloader-with-watchdog-reset/697328)). The building blocks are public: Optiboot's application-request entry, and the QMK keyboard firmware's pattern of a magic value, a forced watchdog reset and an early-startup jump ([QMK bootloader.c mirror](https://git.poz.pet/poz/qmk_firmware/raw/commit/fca31693df15f62cc3fd5851a0d1bae40eb179c2/tmk_core/common/avr/bootloader.c)). Using them so that an ESP32 can flash an AVR over Wi-Fi without a reset wire turned up in **no public project** the research found. It is idea 1 in the final section. MCUdude's optiboot_flash (v7.0-based) has the same entry rule and prebuilt rates up to 1 Mbaud ([optiboot_flash](https://github.com/MCUdude/optiboot_flash)). urboot u8.0, which MiniCore 3.x now ships instead of Optiboot ([MiniCore](https://github.com/MCUdude/MiniCore)), enters **only** on an external reset, and v8.0 dropped `-c arduino` compatibility ([urboot](https://github.com/stefanrueger/urboot)). It would need a source fork, so it is the "build our own" fallback rather than the first choice.

**One Optiboot build option changes recovery.** With `NO_START_APP_ON_POR`, the app starts only after a watchdog reset, so every power-on runs the bootloader first ([Optiboot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)). The ESP32 can then always reach the bootloader, even when the UNO app is broken. The cost is a boot delay equal to the bootloader timeout, which is 1 s by default and can be set to 2, 4 or 8 s on most chips ([Optiboot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)). The catch is that the ESP32 must be talking within that window after power-on, and the ESP32-S3's boot time hasn't been measured. Without the option, a broken UNO app is recovered over USB or with the reset button.

**The robot never needs to update itself without the ESP32.** Both Optiboot 8 and optiboot_flash export a flash-write routine that the application can call ([Optiboot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c), [optiboot_flash.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MCUdude/optiboot_flash/master/optiboot_flash.c)). A 32 KB part with a 512-byte bootloader can stage a second copy only if the app stays under 16,128 bytes (**estimate**), and ArduinoOTA explicitly excludes the ATmega328P for that reason ([ArduinoOTA](https://github.com/JAndrassy/ArduinoOTA)). The ESP32's 8 MB flash is the natural staging area, and the UNO keeps a plain bootloader.

### The one-time ICSP step: UNO R4 WiFi as the programmer, no fuse changes

**Use the spare UNO R4 WiFi running ArduinoISP.** A September 2024 Arduino forum thread confirms that "you can use the UNO R4 WiFi or UNO R4 Minima board as a programmer". The thread's recipe: add `#define USE_OLD_STYLE_WIRING` to ArduinoISP, select "Arduino as ISP (ATmega32U4)", and wire R4 pins 10, 11, 12 and 13 plus 5 V and GND to the target's RESET, MOSI, MISO, SCK, 5 V and GND. avrdude talks STK500v1 at 19200 baud ([Arduino forum 1298093](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/arduino-uno-r4-wifi-as-bootloader-burner/1298093)). This is forum evidence only. Arduino's own ArduinoISP page doesn't list the R4. Uploading any other sketch to the R4 afterwards erases ArduinoISP ([forum reply](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/arduino-uno-r4-wifi-as-bootloader-burner/1298093/2)). The R4 is already owned, and avrdude already ships inside the `arduino:avr` core the project installed ([tools](../tools.md)). The step therefore adds no new tool, apart from the R4's own Arduino core if it isn't installed yet **[unverified: core name and whether arduino-cli can build ArduinoISP without the IDE]**. A USBasp is the fallback. Its newest official firmware is still from 2011, Windows needs a driver installed with Zadig ([fischl.de](https://www.fischl.de/usbasp/)), and many clones ship older firmware that fails with "cannot set sck period" ([Lincomatic](https://blog.lincomatic.com/how-to-update-the-firmware-on-a-usbasp-v2-0/)). That is a firmware lottery plus a Windows driver step, compared with a board already on the desk.

**No fuse change is needed, and that removes most of the bricking risk.** Arduino's UNO definition uses low 0xFF, high 0xDE, extended 0xFD and lock 0x0F, with a 32,256-byte upload limit ([boards.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/arduino/ArduinoCore-avr/master/boards.txt)). A 512-byte Optiboot 8.3 uses the same boot size and reset vector, so the ICSP session only needs to erase the chip, write the bootloader and restore lock 0x0F. The dangerous fuse bits are those that disable reset (RSTDISBL) or serial programming (SPIEN), plus a wrong clock selection. A mistake there locks out ISP, and the only recovery is 12 V high-voltage parallel programming ([Arduino forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/can-a-328p-be-recovered-if-the-spi-fuse-is-set-to-disable/910578), [hinterm-ziel.de](https://hinterm-ziel.de/index.php/2021/07/21/burnt-fuses-and-bricked-mcus/)). The safe recipe never writes the fuses. It reads them, verifies them against the expected values and leaves them alone. The fuses and lock bits of our robot's chip have never been read, so the first command of the session should read them **[unverified that they match the Arduino defaults]**. The chip erase also wipes the application and EEPROM, so read EEPROM first. Nothing here is irreversible: ICSP can put the stock Optiboot 4.4 and the stock application back at any time, which keeps D6 intact.

**The ICSP lines double as robot signals.** MOSI, MISO and SCK are D11 (tilt servo signal), D12 (ultrasonic echo, an output from the sensor) and D13 (ultrasonic trigger) ([pin map](../hardware/pin-map.md)). The echo output would fight MISO, and the servo would see programming traffic. Unplugging the ultrasonic and tilt-servo connectors for the session avoids both, and that isn't a hardware change. Whether the shield leaves the UNO's ICSP header reachable is **[unverified]**. During ICSP the UNO is held in reset, so its pins float and the TB6612's internal 200 kΩ pull-downs keep the driver in standby ([motor driver](../hardware/motor-driver.md)).

**Who builds the bootloader.** No source checked whether upstream ships a prebuilt Optiboot 8.3 hex for the 328P at the chosen baud rate **[unverified]**. Building it needs avr-gcc and make. CI on Linux is the cross-platform answer: it builds the hex from a pinned upstream commit and publishes it, so no contributor needs a local AVR build setup for this one file.

### 250,000 baud is exact on both chips, but only if the USB chip agrees

Error against the AVR's receiver tolerance decides the baud rate. Microchip recommends keeping receiver error within **±2.0%** at normal speed and **±1.5%** in double-speed mode. That figure comes from the AVR DB family's datasheet, which uses the same model as the 328P **[unverified for the 328P itself]** ([Microchip](https://onlinedocs.microchip.com/oxy/GUID-40910A7E-D42A-4D4C-B9C2-D8614E3BC666-en-US-6/GUID-3FD21524-1116-412C-8139-B7FBEA62950A.html)). The table uses the datasheet formula at 16 MHz, plus the flashing time for a 20 KB image. Each 128-byte page costs about 141 bytes on the wire for the write and the same for the read-back, plus about 9 ms of flash erase and write (**estimate**; the page time is a typical datasheet figure, not re-checked). esp-link's measured 3,674 B/s at 57,600 baud ([esp-link](https://github.com/jeelabs/esp-link/blob/master/UC-FLASHING.md)) cross-checks the model.

| Baud | AVR error (best mode) | Write plus verify, 20 KB (**estimate**) |
|---|---|---|
| 57,600 | −0.79% | about 9.3 s |
| 115,200 | +2.12% | about 5.4 s |
| **250,000** | **0.00%** | **about 3.2 s** |
| 500,000 | 0.00% | about 2.3 s |
| 1,000,000 | 0.00% | about 1.9 s |

**Choose 250,000 baud, after two bench checks.** It is exact on the AVR. On the ESP32 it is 80 MHz divided by 320, which also looks exact, but ESP-IDF warns that the real rate can deviate "due to rounding error" and offers `uart_get_baudrate()` to read it back ([ESP-IDF UART](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/peripherals/uart.html)), so it needs checking **[unverified]**. It also matches the stage 3 application baud recommended in the link report. The second check matters for option A. A bootloader built for 250,000 baud must also accept uploads over USB, so the CH340 has to support that rate **[unverified]**. Arduino's stock UNO profile uploads at 115200 ([boards.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/arduino/ArduinoCore-avr/master/boards.txt)), so the project would also need its own board entry or direct avrdude calls. If the CH340 fails at 250k, build the bootloader for 115200 and set the ESP32 to the AVR's real 117,647 baud. Avoid 1 Mbaud, which leaves the AVR only about 10 µs per byte.

**The electrical margin is the open risk.** The UNO's 5 V TX drives GPIO3 directly, and the ESP32-S3 datasheet allows at most VDD + 0.3 V, about 3.6 V ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md#5-v-on-the-uart)). The stock link already does this at 9600 baud all day. Flashing doesn't change the voltage level, but it makes the ESP32 depend on reading every reply correctly. In the other direction, the ESP32's 3.3 V drives the AVR's RX against an input-high threshold of 0.6 × VCC, about 3.0 V, which leaves roughly 0.3 V of margin **[unverified: threshold from memory, not checked against the datasheet]**. **If the GPIO3 measurement shows a fix is needed, that fix is a hardware change, and this decision depends on it.** The rule in [0001](../decisions/0001-stock-hardware-first.md) asks for that to be flagged rather than deferred silently, so it is flagged here. A scope check of edges at 250k and a long run of write-and-verify cycles should come before any rate is committed.

### The ESP32 programmer is ours to write

No maintained ESP32 library programs Optiboot without a reset GPIO. The candidates are small and stale. ESP-UARTArduinoFirmwareUpdate (Apache-2.0) was last pushed in 2023 ([pkerspe](https://github.com/pkerspe/ESP-UARTArduinoFirmwareUpdate)), and the OTA_update_AVR_using_ESP32 project (MIT) in 2020 ([ESP32-Musings](https://github.com/ESP32-Musings/OTA_update_AVR_using_ESP32)). esp-link is a mature protocol reference, but it targets the ESP8266, and its master branch is "currently unstable" ([esp-link](https://github.com/jeelabs/esp-link)). **Building our own STK500v1 host on the ESP32 is the recommended path**, at an estimated 200 to 400 lines plus host tests. It sends the enter command at the application baud, switches its UART to the bootloader baud, repeats sync every 50 to 80 ms for up to 2 s, writes pages, reads every page back, and leaves the bootloader only after a full verify. STK500v1 has no checksum, only in-sync and OK bytes, so the read-back is mandatory. The alternative is a transparent TCP bridge driven by desktop avrdude (`-P net:host:port`, [avrdude 8.1 manual](https://avrdudes.github.io/avrdude/8.1/avrdude_4.html)). That is simpler on the ESP32, but each page then pays several Wi-Fi round trips, about 13 s extra at 20 ms each (**estimate**). It also puts the bootloader's 1 s timeout at the mercy of the network. It is a reasonable bench tool and a poor production path.

### Keeping the motors off while the UNO is flashed (SG3)

SG3 names flashing explicitly as a time the robot must not move ([requirements](../requirements.md)). After any true reset every AVR pin is a high-impedance input, and the TB6612's pull-downs on STBY, PWM and IN hold the driver in standby ([motor driver](../hardware/motor-driver.md)). The WS2812 line has a 100 kΩ pull-down ([pin map](../hardware/pin-map.md)). Optiboot drives only TX and its LED pin, PB5. On this robot PB5 is D13, the ultrasonic trigger, which is harmless ([optiboot_flash](https://github.com/MCUdude/optiboot_flash)). The safe sequence is short. The ESP32 accepts an update only when the robot is stopped and the battery is above S9's 7.0 V warning level ([requirements](../requirements.md)). It refuses drive commands until the new UNO firmware reports its version. The UNO drives STBY and PWM low and then restarts through the watchdog, never through a direct jump. Three risks remain. The servo lines (D10, D11) float, and a servo can twitch, which hasn't been tested on this robot. A half-written image could run, which is handled below. Bench work should keep the tracks off the ground until both are measured.

**A power cut can't damage the bootloader, but it can leave a half-written application.** The stock lock value 0x0F stops the application area from writing the boot section, and the reset vector points at the bootloader ([boards.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/arduino/ArduinoCore-avr/master/boards.txt)). Optiboot writes page by page with no chip erase ([Optiboot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)), so an interrupted update leaves a mix of old and new pages, and running that mix could do anything, including enabling the motors. The proposed protection is a write order that keeps any reset during the update in the bootloader until the image is complete and verified. It shrinks the unsafe window from the whole transfer (about 3 s) to two single-page writes of roughly 10 ms each (**estimate**). It is idea 2 in the final section. The ESP32 also keeps the previous UNO image, so any failure ends in a re-flash, never in a guess.

## The ESP32-S3 already has two slots, and rollback takes one deliberate hook

**Keep the factory partition table.** Our measured layout is exactly Arduino-ESP32's `default_8MB.csv`: NVS, otadata, two 3.2 MB app slots, 1.5 MB SPIFFS and a core-dump area ([default_8MB.csv](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/blob/3.3.12/tools/partitions/default_8MB.csv), [camera board backup](../notebook/2026-11-06-backing-up-the-camera-board.md), measured with esptool 5.6.0). A camera and Wi-Fi app fits easily, and the SPIFFS partition can hold many UNO images, formatted as LittleFS under the same label (**[unverified]** that the Arduino LittleFS library mounts it without a table change). ESP-IDF calls updating the bootloader or partition table over the air an "unsafe" mode, because power loss during the final copy "can leave the device unrecoverable". The safe bootloader update needs a ROM recovery feature that the ESP32-S3 doesn't have ([ESP-IDF OTA](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/ota.html), [esp_rom](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/tree/master/components/esp_rom)). **Bootloader and partition-table changes therefore go over USB only.** The last-resort recovery is ROM download mode (hold BOOT, press RESET), which works on our board even though esptool's automatic reset doesn't ([camera board backup](../notebook/2026-11-06-backing-up-the-camera-board.md), measured). It must never be disabled by eFuse. The USB-C port's 5 V feeds the shield's 5 V rail directly, so the 4-pin cable comes off before USB-C goes on ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md)).

**Rollback is built into Arduino, but by default it confirms everything.** ESP-IDF's rollback marks a new app `PENDING_VERIFY` on its first boot. If the app is still unconfirmed at the next boot, after a crash, watchdog reset or power loss, the bootloader boots the previous app. The states live in otadata, which survives power loss ([ESP-IDF OTA](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/ota.html)). Arduino's precompiled bootloader and libraries enable rollback in both the 2.0.x and 3.x builds ([lib-builder defconfig](https://github.com/espressif/esp32-arduino-lib-builder/blob/master/configs/defconfig.common)). But the 3.3.12 core's startup code calls a weak `verifyOta()` that returns true and marks the app valid **before `setup()` runs** ([esp32-hal-misc.c](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/blob/3.3.12/cores/esp32/esp32-hal-misc.c)). Our firmware must override `verifyRollbackLater()` to return true. It should then confirm itself only after a real health check: Wi-Fi up, camera started, the UNO answering, and several seconds without a crash. Otherwise an app that crashes in `setup()` is already "valid" and never rolls back. Which bootloader the factory image carries is unknown, but our first USB flash with Arduino 3.x installs the 3.x bootloader, which has rollback on. OTA never replaces the bootloader, so the factory one stays until then ([platform.txt](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/blob/3.3.12/platform.txt)).

**Which stack:** Arduino-ESP32 **3.3.12** (on ESP-IDF 5.5.5, September 2026) is the current stable core. 4.0.0-RC1 (on ESP-IDF 6.1) is a pre-release ([arduino-esp32 releases](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/releases)). The core's `Update` library already has SHA-256 checks, `installSignature()` and `rollBack()` ([Update.h](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/blob/3.3.12/libraries/Update/src/Update.h)). Several security options in this report are ESP-IDF build settings that the precompiled core fixes and can't change: software-signed apps, anti-rollback, Secure Boot, HMAC-based NVS encryption and reproducible builds. Espressif's FAQ says the only way to get them with Arduino is "Arduino as an IDF component" ([ESP-FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-faq/en/latest/software-framework/security.html)). The recommended design below works on the precompiled core, which keeps that framework choice open.

**The computer tool should tell the robot to pull the update.** The tool queries the robot's versions, free space, motor state and battery. It serves the release from a short-lived local HTTP server and sends an authenticated "update from this URL, expecting this manifest" command. The robot downloads, verifies and only then switches slots, and a failed download leaves the running app untouched. Plain HTTP is acceptable because trust comes from the signature, not the transport. ESP-IDF's own HTTPS OTA labels plain HTTP "ONLY FOR TESTING" ([Kconfig](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/blob/v6.1/components/esp_https_ota/Kconfig)), which is a reminder that without a signature check HTTP would be unsafe.

| Delivery model | How it works | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| espota (ArduinoOTA) | The PC sends a UDP invite. The robot connects back to the PC over TCP. Optional PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 password challenge ([ArduinoOTA.cpp](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/blob/3.3.12/libraries/ArduinoOTA/src/ArduinoOTA.cpp)) | Bench development only. Desktop firewalls often block the inbound connection |
| HTTP POST push (OTAWebUpdater, ElegantOTA) | An always-on upload page. Arduino's doc example uses admin/admin with no warning ([OTA Web Update](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/arduino-esp32/en/latest/ota_web_update.html)). ElegantOTA Lite is AGPL-3.0 ([ElegantOTA](https://github.com/ayushsharma82/ElegantOTA)) | No. A permanent attack surface, and AGPL is incompatible with Apache-2.0 firmware |
| **Tool-initiated pull** | An authenticated command tells the robot where to download. `HTTPUpdate` or `esp_https_ota` can check the version first and resume via HTTP Range ([esp_https_ota](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/esp_https_ota.html)) | **Recommended** |

**Expect well under a minute for a full two-board release.** The ESP32-S3 manages about 20 Mbit/s of TCP in open air ([ESP-IDF Wi-Fi performance](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-guides/wifi-driver/wifi-performance-and-power-save.html)), so a 1.5 MB image takes about a second on the wire. Flash erase, reboot and reconnect dominate, and a target under about 30 s for the ESP32 image is reasonable (**estimate**). A comparable two-chip project cut its update from 228 s to 26 s, through 64 KB erase blocks, 4 KB transfer blocks instead of 256-byte ones, and a faster UART ([Scope Creep Labs](https://scopecreeplabs-site.pages.dev/blog/stm32-esp32-ota)). Forum threads also report ESP32-S3 updates taking several minutes, with unclear causes. Those pages couldn't be fetched, so they serve only as a warning to measure.

**Skip anti-rollback.** It needs a custom ESP-IDF build. It burns one eFuse bit per security-version step, with only about 16 steps available **[IRREVERSIBLE per step]** ([Kconfig.app_rollback](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/blob/v6.1/components/bootloader/Kconfig.app_rollback)). It also refuses to roll back to an older security version, which would break the two-board recovery below. Without Secure Boot it adds little, because USB can flash anything anyway. A sequence number in the signed manifest, checked in software, blocks Wi-Fi downgrades without spending fuses.

**One release, two boards: "always recoverable to a matched pair".** True atomic updates across two chips aren't possible here. The ESP32 has two slots, but the UNO has one. A coordinated scheme can still guarantee that the robot always ends up with a matched pair: both new or both old. It uses the ESP32's unconfirmed first boot as the transaction window. The new ESP32 app flashes the UNO and confirms itself only once the UNO reports the expected version. If anything fails, it re-flashes the previous UNO image and rolls itself back. The research found no library or published design that does this for an ESP32 and an AVR. The closest published two-chip design states plainly that it is "not fully atomic" ([Scope Creep Labs](https://scopecreeplabs-site.pages.dev/blog/stm32-esp32-ota)). The scheme is idea 3 in the final section. One property matters for planning either way: **the recovery logic must ship in the first release that relies on it**, because after a crash it is the *old* ESP32 firmware that has to repair the UNO.

## One signed manifest covers both boards without burning a fuse

### Why a manifest, and what goes in it

S12 asks that only firmware we have signed can be installed over Wi-Fi, and it notes that the UNO has no secure boot or key storage ([requirements](../requirements.md)). The ATmega328P's lock bits don't help. They restrict only external programming and readout, they don't stop the bootloader from rewriting the app, and only a chip erase clears them ([Microchip lock bits](https://onlinedocs.microchip.com/oxy/GUID-0EC909F9-8FB7-46B2-BF4B-05290662B5C3-en-US-12.1.1/GUID-9685E592-1B74-4AFA-B633-4D7A2988EBBB.html)). For open-source firmware, readout protection protects nothing. Leave the readout and ISP lock bits unprogrammed, keep the stock boot-section protection (lock 0x0F), and record why. The ESP32 is therefore the gatekeeper for both boards. It verifies one signature over a release manifest, checks each image's SHA-256 against it, and refuses anything else over Wi-Fi. USB stays open on both boards, which is acceptable because USB means physical presence. D15 and D16 fall out of the same file. D16 wants a numbered package listing every image and its checksum, which the robot can report back. D15 wants mismatched parts rejected by the bootloader or the flashing tool ([requirements](../requirements.md)). On this robot the ESP32 is the flashing tool for both boards.

Existing formats each fit badly on their own. **SUIT** (CBOR and COSE) is the standards answer, but its manifest draft (-38) was still in the RFC Editor queue in November 2026 ([IETF datatracker](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-suit-manifest/)), and it is heavy for a hobby project. **MCUboot's** TLV format has the best compatibility primitives (dependency, security counter, vendor and class IDs), but it belongs to a bootloader we wouldn't use ([MCUboot design](https://docs.mcuboot.com/design.html)). **Uptane**, built for vehicle updates, contributes the hardware identifier that stops an image reaching the wrong model of controller ([Uptane Targets module](https://github.com/uptane/asn1/blob/master/TargetsModule.asn1)). **Building our own small signed JSON manifest** is the recommended option. It borrows SUIT's field names (vendor ID, class ID, component ID, sequence number, image digest), Uptane's hardware identifier and MCUboot's dependency rule, so a later move to SUIT stays cheap. The estimated cost is a schema, a signer in the computer tool and a verifier of a few hundred lines on the ESP32. The manifest carries the release number and a monotonically increasing sequence number. It also lists each component's ID, size, SHA-256, version and target chip, and the link-protocol version. To stay consistent with the link report, it should include the 4-byte hash of the protocol description file and the calibration-set schema hash, so calibration sets from another release are rejected too. Each firmware also reports its protocol version and description hash at runtime. A mismatched pair is then caught even when someone flashes the UNO over USB by hand, because the ESP32 refuses motion commands until the pair matches.

### Choosing a signature

| Option | Burns eFuses? | Works on precompiled Arduino? | Algorithm | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hardware Secure Boot V2 | **Yes [IRREVERSIBLE]** | No. Needs Arduino as an IDF component ([ESP-FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-faq/en/latest/software-framework/security.html)) | RSA-3072 PSS only on the S3 ([Secure Boot V2](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/secure-boot-v2.html)) | Locks the robot to the key holder. See the eFuse section |
| ESP-IDF signed apps without Secure Boot | No | No (a build setting) | RSA-3072, the same format as Secure Boot | Checks OTA images against the key in the running app. "Does not protect against an attacker with flash write access" ([Secure Boot V2](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/secure-boot-v2.html)). Covers the ESP32 image only |
| Arduino `Update.installSignature()` / `ArduinoOTA.setSignature()` | No | Yes. The signed-OTA PR merged in December 2025 with RSA or ECDSA ([PR #11968](https://github.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/pull/11968)) | RSA or ECDSA | The release it shipped in and its exact algorithms are **[unverified]**. Covers the ESP32 image only |
| **Our own signed manifest (Ed25519)** | No | Yes | Ed25519 via Monocypher, under 2,000 lines, CC0 or BSD-2 ([Monocypher](https://monocypher.org/)) | **Recommended.** One signature covers both images and the compatibility data |
| Our own signed manifest (RSA-3072) | No | Yes | RSA-3072 PSS | Keeps the same key usable if hardware Secure Boot is ever enabled |

**Ed25519 is the recommended default.** It has small keys and a simple, auditable verifier, and the minisign format already defines a signature file with a "trusted comment" that can carry the release and sequence number ([minisign](https://jedisct1.github.io/minisign/)). RSA-3072 is the choice if the maintainer wants to keep hardware Secure Boot as a real option, because the S3 supports no other algorithm for it. One older forum report describes ESP-IDF signature verification passing with a corrupted certificate ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=68363)). Whatever the choice, the test suite must include negative cases: a wrong key, a flipped bit, an old sequence number and a wrong hardware ID. Each must be rejected.

### Keys that fit an open-source robot

The release key stays out of the repo and off CI by default. A password-protected key on an offline USB stick is practical, because minisign encrypts secret keys with scrypt ([minisign](https://jedisct1.github.io/minisign/)). A CI signing key behind a GitHub environment with required reviewers is the optional convenience step. Forks keep their freedom. Because verification is a software policy, flashing a fork's build over USB re-keys that robot, and OTA from then on accepts the fork's key. That matches ESP-IDF's own software-only model, which trusts "the key embedded in the running app" ([Secure Boot V2](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/secure-boot-v2.html)). Provenance comes later. GitHub artifact attestations are free on public repositories and need Enterprise Cloud on private ones ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/actions/security-guides/using-artifact-attestations-to-establish-provenance-for-builds)), so they become available when the repo goes public under [0005](../decisions/0005-publish-with-first-working-firmware.md). ESP-IDF's reproducible-build option strips dates and paths, but the output still depends on the exact IDF, CMake, Ninja and compiler versions ([ESP-IDF reproducible builds](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.1/esp32s3/api-guides/reproducible-builds.html)). Like the other IDF options, it may not be settable on the precompiled Arduino core **[unverified]**.

### Every irreversible eFuse step, and why none should be burned now

An ESP32-S3 eFuse is a bit that "can be programmed to 1 after which it cannot be reverted back to 0", and the key blocks can be written only once ([eFuse manager](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/efuse.html)). The chip has six 256-bit key blocks, shared between Secure Boot digests, flash-encryption keys and HMAC keys ([eFuse manager](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/efuse.html)). Our board's eFuse state has never been read. `espefuse summary`, which comes with the esptool already installed, reads it without changing anything ([eFuse manager](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/efuse.html)), and that read should come before any further discussion.

| Step | What it permanently does | Cost to this project | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Secure Boot V2 enable** [IRREVERSIBLE] | Burns a key digest into a key block and sets SECURE_BOOT_EN. From then on, only images signed by an enrolled key boot. The bootloader can no longer be updated over the air. The ROM USB-OTG stack is disabled ([Secure Boot V2](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/secure-boot-v2.html)) | The owner and forks can never flash their own unsigned builds again. Needs a custom IDF build | **Don't** |
| **Key revocation** [IRREVERSIBLE] | KEY_REVOKEx retires a key slot forever. "Aggressive" revocation retires a key on any failed check, and Espressif warns it "could also brick the device permanently" ([Secure Boot V2](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/secure-boot-v2.html)) | One bad flash could revoke the only key | **Don't** |
| **Flash-encryption key** [IRREVERSIBLE] | Uses one or two key blocks. A host-generated key can be burned "only once" ([Flash Encryption](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/flash-encryption.html)) | Protects nothing for open-source firmware. NVS can't be flash-encrypted anyway | **Don't** |
| **Flash encryption, development mode** [IRREVERSIBLE, partly] | Burns cache and JTAG disables at first boot. Encryption can be turned off **only once per chip** ([Flash Encryption](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/flash-encryption.html)) | Loses JTAG debugging, which D12 needs | **Don't** |
| **Flash encryption, release mode** [IRREVERSIBLE] | Write-protects the encryption counter. Plaintext can then arrive only by OTA ([Flash Encryption](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/flash-encryption.html)) | Ends serial flashing | **Don't** |
| **JTAG disable** (HARD_DIS_JTAG, DIS_USB_JTAG, DIS_PAD_JTAG) [IRREVERSIBLE] | Turns off hardware debugging. Only the soft variant can be re-enabled, with an HMAC key ([Security overview](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/security.html)) | Breaks D12 | **Don't** |
| **Download-mode lockdown** (DIS_DOWNLOAD_MODE, ENABLE_SECURITY_DOWNLOAD, DIS_USB_SERIAL_JTAG_DOWNLOAD_MODE) [IRREVERSIBLE] | Removes or restricts ROM download mode. After ENABLE_SECURITY_DOWNLOAD, no more eFuses can be burned over the port ([Enablement workflows](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/security/security-features-enablement-workflows.html)) | Removes the last-resort USB recovery | **Never** |
| **Anti-rollback SECURE_VERSION** [IRREVERSIBLE per step] | One bit per step, about 16 steps ([ESP-IDF OTA](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/ota.html)) | Breaks the two-board recovery. Runs out quickly | **Don't** |
| **HMAC key for NVS encryption** [IRREVERSIBLE: uses one key block] | Encrypts NVS, including the Wi-Fi password, with keys derived from an eFuse key. **Doesn't need flash encryption.** If the block is empty, the firmware generates and burns the key itself ([NVS Encryption](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/storage/nvs_encryption.html)) | Locks nothing out, but needs a custom IDF build. Without Secure Boot, a malicious app could still use the key | **The only candidate.** Decide later |

Any future eFuse experiment should be rehearsed first with virtual eFuses. `CONFIG_EFUSE_VIRTUAL`, optionally kept in flash across reboots, emulates burns without touching the chip ([eFuse manager](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/efuse.html)). Any real experiment belongs on a spare module, never the maintainer's robot.

## Pair once over USB, then authenticate every command

### Getting the Wi-Fi password onto the robot

The robot has no screen or keypad, and the first flash of our camera firmware has to happen over USB anyway. That makes **pairing over USB in the computer tool** the natural main path. While the camera board is on USB, the tool writes the home SSID and password and generates a random 32-byte link key. Both go into the robot's NVS, and the link key is also stored in the computer's per-user config folder, never in the repo. MAVLink's signing guide gives the same rule: keys are set "only over a secure link" such as USB, and never broadcast ([mavlink.io](https://mavlink.io/en/guide/message_signing.html)). It works on all three operating systems, because it is plain serial from the Python tool. Its cost is a physical step per robot, and the 4-pin cable must come off before USB-C goes on ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md)).

| Method | Security | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| **USB pairing in our computer tool** | Physical presence. Keys never cross the air | **Recommended main path.** Build our own; small |
| Improv Wi-Fi over serial | Physical presence. Browser use needs Chrome or Edge, and it doesn't work on iOS ([Improv](https://www.improv-wifi.com/)) | A good protocol to be compatible with. Adopted by WLED, Tasmota and ESPHome |
| Improv over BLE | No credential encryption in the spec. Only an optional button-press authorization ([Improv BLE](https://www.improv-wifi.com/ble/)) | Only with a physical step, such as the MODE button **[untested idea]** |
| Espressif `network_provisioning`, security level 2 | SRP6a plus AES-256-GCM, with a per-device password from a sticker or QR code ([ESP-IDF provisioning](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-reference/provisioning/provisioning.html)) | The strongest phone path. BLE costs about 110 KB of RAM. Moved out of IDF into a separate component in v6.0 ([migration guide](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v6.0/esp32/migration-guides/release-6.x/6.0/provisioning.html)) |
| WiFiManager captive portal | Plain HTTP portal behind an AP password ([WiFiManager](https://github.com/tzapu/WiFiManager)) | No. 606 open issues, and the README says its docs are out of date |
| Git-ignored secrets file compiled in | Credentials end up inside every binary | Developer shortcut only, never a release path ([ESPHome](https://esphome.io/guides/security_best_practices/)) |

**A fallback access point, if there is one, gets a per-robot password and very little power.** ESPHome's guide says to "ALWAYS SET" the fallback AP's password, and to remove the fallback AP entirely in production ([ESPHome](https://esphome.io/guides/security_best_practices/)). WLED's fixed default AP password is the cautionary example ([WLED security](https://kno.wled.ge/advanced/security)). In open-source firmware, any fixed default can be read straight from the source. A fallback AP should use WPA2/WPA3 mixed mode, which ESP-IDF supports with protected management frames required for WPA3 ([ESP-IDF Wi-Fi security](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v6.0.3/api-guides/wifi-security.html)). It should use a random password generated at pairing, time out, and offer only provisioning and status, never driving. A leaked AP password then can't move the robot.

**Credential storage stays plaintext for now.** The Wi-Fi driver stores the SSID and password in the default NVS partition, unencrypted unless NVS encryption is on ([NVS Encryption](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/storage/nvs_encryption.html)). Anyone holding the robot and a USB cable can dump them. The only fix that doesn't lock anyone out is HMAC-based NVS encryption, which costs one eFuse key block **[IRREVERSIBLE]** and a custom IDF build (see the eFuse table). Accepting the plaintext risk and documenting it is the recommendation for now. Most hobby projects make the same trade, and ESPHome's security guide doesn't ask for NVS encryption ([ESPHome](https://esphome.io/guides/security_best_practices/)).

### Authenticating the control link

S11 asks that the link accept commands only from our tool or page, and SG8 that only the owner can control the robot ([requirements](../requirements.md)). Authentication is the requirement. Confidentiality is a bonus, mostly for the video. Comparable systems split two ways. Drone autopilots sign without encrypting. MAVLink 2 appends a 13-byte block with a link ID, a 48-bit timestamp and a 48-bit truncated SHA-256 MAC under a 32-byte key, and it rejects anything older than the last packet or more than a minute behind ([mavlink.io](https://mavlink.io/en/guide/message_signing.html)). PX4 states the limit plainly: "authentication and integrity only. Messages are still sent in plaintext" ([PX4](https://docs.px4.io/main/en/mavlink/message_signing)). ESPHome encrypts and authenticates with the Noise `NNpsk0` handshake under a 32-byte shared key. It maintains a microcontroller port of noise-c on the Espressif component registry, and Python and JavaScript clients exist ([ESPHome API](https://esphome.io/components/api/), [esphome/noise-c](https://github.com/esphome/noise-c)).

| Option | Gives | Fits the link report's transports? | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Per-packet HMAC-SHA256 with a session nonce and counter** (MAVLink-style) | Authentication of both directions, replay protection. No confidentiality | **Yes.** Each UDP datagram stands alone, and lost or late packets are simply dropped | Smallest. Build our own from standard primitives. Needs a design review |
| Noise `NNpsk0` (ESPHome-style) | Authentication, encryption, fresh keys per session ([Noise spec](https://noiseprotocol.org/noise.html)) | Fits TCP and WebSocket. Over lossy UDP, Noise's implicit message counter needs extra handling **[unverified]** | A maintained component, but its RAM and flash on the S3 are unmeasured |
| TLS 1.3 / WSS | Everything, with certificates | Browsers distrust a self-signed robot certificate | 22 to 42 KB of heap per connection in Espressif's client example ([ESP-IDF mbedTLS](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-reference/protocols/mbedtls.html)) |

**Recommended: per-packet HMAC now, Noise for the video stream if confidentiality matters later.** The link report already puts a sequence number in every packet and sends one packet per UDP datagram. A full-length or 128-bit HMAC tag under a key derived from the pairing key and a random per-session nonce from the robot fits that design directly. The robot has no real-time clock, so MAVLink's wall-clock window doesn't transfer. A session nonce plus a strictly increasing counter replaces it without storing state across reboots. That is a design inference, and it needs an outside review before it is trusted. The UNO's own 300 ms timeout remains the safety backstop, so a dropped or rejected command stops the robot rather than leaving it running. If the maintainer prefers Noise, the link report's decision 6 should lean towards WebSocket for both the tool and the browser, because Noise as specified expects an ordered stream.

**The browser page is the awkward part.** A page served over `http://` from a LAN address or a `.local` name isn't a secure context, so `crypto.subtle` is unavailable ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Security/Secure_Contexts)). A robot-served page would need a pure-JavaScript HMAC, and an active attacker on the LAN could tamper with the page itself. Since Chrome 142, a page on a public HTTPS site needs a permission prompt to reach the LAN, which rules out hosting the control page on GitHub Pages without friction ([Chrome LNA](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/local-network-access)). A page served by the robot to itself is not gated. The alternative is for the computer tool to serve the page on `http://localhost`, which is a secure context ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Security/Secure_Contexts)), and act as the authenticated bridge. That excludes phones, which [0004](../decisions/0004-own-protocol-and-control-page.md) wants to support. ESPHome and WLED both accept plain HTTP on the LAN behind a password or PIN ([ESPHome](https://esphome.io/guides/security_best_practices/), [WLED](https://kno.wled.ge/advanced/security)).

**Stage 2 is exposed, and the switch is the mitigation.** Until our camera firmware replaces it, the stock firmware runs an open AP with an unauthenticated control port 100 ([camera board](../hardware/camera-board.md)), so anyone in range can send commands to the UNO. In Upload, though, the camera board is disconnected from the UNO's RX ([pin map](../hardware/pin-map.md)). During USB bench work the switch is therefore a physical "Wi-Fi control off" switch. Whether the stock camera firmware also accepts firmware over the air is unknown. If it does, S12 is already violated in stage 2.

### Threat model

| Threat | Protection | What remains |
|---|---|---|
| A neighbour in Wi-Fi range (stock firmware today: open AP, port 100) | Station mode on the home WPA2/WPA3 network. A fallback AP only with a per-robot password, a timeout and no driving | Stage 2 stays exposed. Mitigated by the switch |
| A malicious device on the home LAN | Every command authenticated with the per-robot key. No unauthenticated ports. Updates accepted only with a valid manifest signature | mDNS can be spoofed, so discovery never counts as identity |
| Eavesdropping on the LAN | Optional Noise or TLS, mainly for video | Control traffic visible under the HMAC option |
| Replayed commands | Session nonce plus counter. The UNO's 300 ms stop timer | None of note |
| A malicious update over Wi-Fi | Signed manifest, per-image SHA-256, sequence number, hardware ID | The signing key's custody |
| Physical access with a USB cable | Accepted by design: USB is the owner's path | Wi-Fi password readable from flash unless NVS is encrypted |
| Credentials leaking through the repo | Pairing at runtime, git-ignored local files, a secret scanner | Developer builds with compiled-in secrets are sensitive files |

## Decisions for the maintainer

1. **The stage conflict:** move D2, D3 and S12 to stage 3, or amend 0004 to bring a minimal camera firmware (Wi-Fi, OTA, UNO programmer, bridge) into stage 2.
2. **The path:** confirm option A now and option B as the target, and whether to use the reset-button bridge if the ICSP step is delayed.
3. **The ICSP session:** approve it as a robot session, and whether it also reads the fuses, lock bits and EEPROM to complete the D6 backup.
4. **Programmer:** the UNO R4 WiFi running ArduinoISP (recommended) or a USBasp.
5. **Bootloader:** upstream Optiboot 8.3 built in CI (recommended), MCUdude's optiboot_flash, or a urboot fork.
6. **Bootloader baud:** 250,000 (recommended, if the CH340 and ESP32 bench checks pass) or 115,200 with the ESP32 matched to 117,647.
7. **Power-on behaviour:** build with `NO_START_APP_ON_POR` and a 2 to 4 s window (always recoverable over Wi-Fi, with a boot delay) or keep the default (no delay, USB or reset-button recovery).
8. **ESP32 framework:** the precompiled Arduino-ESP32 3.3.x, moving to 4.0 when stable (recommended for now), or Arduino as an ESP-IDF component, which is needed for any IDF-only security option.
9. **Update delivery:** tool-initiated pull (recommended), with espota allowed on the bench only.
10. **Signature algorithm:** Ed25519 over our own manifest (recommended), or RSA-3072 to keep hardware Secure Boot open.
11. **Keys:** an offline release key, whether to add a CI signing key behind a protected environment, and the fork and re-key policy over USB.
12. **Downgrades:** reject lower sequence numbers over Wi-Fi and allow downgrades only over USB (recommended), or allow signed downgrades.
13. **eFuses:** confirm "burn nothing" as policy, and whether HMAC-based NVS encryption (one key block, irreversible) is ever wanted.
14. **Provisioning:** USB pairing in the computer tool (recommended), whether to make it Improv-serial compatible, and whether a phone path (Espressif security level 2) is needed later.
15. **Fallback AP:** none, or one with a per-robot password, a timeout and no driving.
16. **Link authentication:** per-packet HMAC with a session nonce and counter (recommended, fits UDP) or Noise `NNpsk0` (which pushes the link report's decision 6 towards WebSocket).
17. **Browser page:** served by the robot over HTTP with pure-JavaScript authentication (phones work, LAN tampering is a residual risk), or served by the computer tool on localhost (secure context, no phones).
18. **Stage 2 exposure:** accept the stock open AP until stage 3, with the switch in Upload during USB bench work.
19. **Novel ideas:** whether to get patent advice before any of the three ideas below reaches the repo, since 0005 makes the whole history public. *Decided on 2026-11-16: published openly as novelties N1 to N3.*

## Conclusion

The missing reset wire looked like a hardware problem, and it turns out to be a bootloader policy problem. Optiboot 4.4 trusts only an external reset, and a 512-byte bootloader that also trusts the application removes the need for a wire, with nothing soldered and no fuse touched. The same pattern holds across the report. Each "can't" on stock hardware traced back to a default that could be changed: Arduino's automatic rollback confirmation, the stock firmware's open AP, the assumption that signing means Secure Boot. Each default can be changed in software and changed back. The irreversible features are the ones to leave alone, because their main effect on a single-owner open-source robot is to take power away from its owner.

The deeper shift is where trust lives. With no cryptography on the UNO and no fuses burned on the ESP32, the release manifest becomes the robot's security boundary, verified by the one chip that can check a signature and enforced over the one path (Wi-Fi) that an outsider can reach. USB stays the owner's door on purpose. That is a cleaner match for SG8 than hardware locking. It also means the manifest schema, the signer and the ESP32's verifier deserve the same review discipline the project gives the safety code.

## Possibly novel ideas

These ideas came out of the research. The notes found no public project or document that describes them, but the search covered public open-source projects and documentation only, and no patent databases were searched. On 2026-11-16 the maintainer decided to publish them openly, as novelties [N1](../novelties/N1-flashing-without-a-reset-wire.md), [N2](../novelties/N2-a-half-written-image-never-runs.md) and [N3](../novelties/N3-two-boards-that-always-match.md).

**Idea 1: wireless AVR flashing with no reset wire, through an application-requested bootloader entry.** The UNO's firmware, on an authenticated command from the ESP32, makes the robot safe and restarts through a real watchdog reset into a bootloader built to accept such a request. The ESP32 then programs it over the shared UART. *Public building blocks:* Optiboot's application-request entry ([Optiboot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Optiboot/optiboot/master/optiboot/bootloaders/optiboot/optiboot.c)), QMK's watchdog-then-jump pattern ([QMK](https://git.poz.pet/poz/qmk_firmware/raw/commit/fca31693df15f62cc3fd5851a0d1bae40eb179c2/tmk_core/common/avr/bootloader.c)), and esp-link's Wi-Fi flashing with a wired reset ([esp-link](https://github.com/jeelabs/esp-link/blob/master/UC-FLASHING.md)). *Not found:* any project that combines them so a Wi-Fi co-processor flashes an AVR without a reset line. *Needed to decide:* option B depends on it.

**Idea 2: a page-write order that keeps a half-written AVR image from ever running.** The programmer first writes a tiny placeholder into the first page that sends any start of the application straight back to the bootloader. It then writes and verifies the rest of the image, and replaces the placeholder with the real first page last. A reset at any point in between lands back in the bootloader with all outputs off, still reachable over the UART. Two short windows remain, each about one page write long (**estimate**), when the first page itself is being rewritten. *Public building blocks:* Optiboot's page-by-page writes, the AVR's reset vector pointing at the bootloader ([boards.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/arduino/ArduinoCore-avr/master/boards.txt)), and urboot's option that protects the reset vector ([urboot](https://github.com/stefanrueger/urboot)). *Not found:* a published power-fail scheme for Optiboot updates, or any measurement of Optiboot's power-fail behaviour. *Needed to decide:* option B's power-fail claim rests on it.

**Idea 3: a two-board release that always ends as a matched pair, using the ESP32's rollback window as the transaction.** The new ESP32 app stays unconfirmed while it flashes and checks the UNO. It confirms itself only when the UNO reports the expected version. On any failure, or after a crash or power cut, the ESP32 restores the previous UNO image and the previous ESP32 app. *Public building blocks:* ESP-IDF's rollback states ([ESP-IDF OTA](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32s3/api-reference/system/ota.html)), and a published ESP32-plus-STM32 updater that its author calls "not fully atomic" ([Scope Creep Labs](https://scopecreeplabs-site.pages.dev/blog/stm32-esp32-ota)). *Not found:* a library or design that coordinates an ESP32 and a single-slot AVR this way. *Needed to decide:* D16's "flashing a release installs exactly that".

The other designs in this report are established practice and carry no such caution: the signed manifest borrowing from SUIT, Uptane and MCUboot, USB pairing, per-packet HMAC in the style of MAVLink, and the tool-initiated pull.
